              Every named figure in the poem

                Homer records no descent inside this house — its figures enter the poem without a line. They are listed in full beside the chart.

                Each row is one generation. The bronze bracket is descent: it falls from a marriage to a bar the children hang from, and is dashed where a line skips a generation. The red rule joins a married pair; someone who married into the poem stands on their partner’s row. Hover or tab to a figure to light its whole line.

                  The reckoning of days

                  Forty days, holding ten years

                  The Odyssey’s present action runs about forty days. Almost half the poem happens inside four of them, and the ten years of wandering are told in a single evening by the only man who saw them.

                  How to read the reckoning

                  A poem that tells itself out of order

                  Homer starts in the tenth year of a ten-year return, spends four books on a son who has not met his father, and lets the hero appear in Book V as a man sitting on a beach crying. The wanderings everyone remembers — the Cyclops, Circe, the dead, the Sirens — are a flashback narrated at a dinner party by the only surviving witness.

                  This companion therefore keeps two clocks. The day count is the poem’s present, about forty days from the council on Olympus to the truce in Laertes’ orchard. The told order is the order you read in. Where a book reports events from outside its own day, the interface says whose report it is, because in this poem that is rarely a neutral fact.
